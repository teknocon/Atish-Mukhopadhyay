About the Artist

Sarode Exponent of the
Maihar Seniya Gharana

I am an India-born Sarode player and music educator carrying the legacy of the Maihar Seniya Gharana, one of the most revered lineages in North Indian Classical music, rooted in the 16th century. I received nearly 40 years of rigorous training in the traditional Guru-Shishya Parampara, under the guidance of world-renowned masters.

This immersive process shaped me both technically and aesthetically, and continues to guide my musical and teaching philosophy.

As an educator, I have taught a wide range of North Indian classical instruments, along with North Indian Classical Vocal Music. I have also mentored students who play non-Indian instruments such as Violin, Cello, Guitar, and Oud, helping them apply North Indian classical language to their own musical backgrounds. This versatility allows me to connect with students across cultures.

In 2012, I was invited to the Tchaikovsky Conservatory in Moscow, where I launched and led a regular North Indian Classical Music course for over a decade. On its 10th anniversary, I received the Nikolai Rubinstein Medal. I also co-founded the Aashish Khan School of World Music in India, serving as curriculum developer and teacher for nearly ten years.

I have taught students of all ages and cultural backgrounds for over 20 years, both privately and in institutions. My lecture-demonstrations include York University, University of Pennsylvania, and The Royal Conservatory of Music.

As a performer, I've played over 600 Sarode concerts and international music festivals worldwide, including collaborations with Grammy-nominated musicians. One of my works was selected by the National Gallery Prague for their permanent exhibition "Art of Asia" (2025).

I also volunteered for the EU-funded "Children of War: Invisible Wounds" project in Ukraine, conducting webinars and distributing a Sarode CD to aid traumatized children in war zones.

From the Heart

Artist Statement

"

As a lifelong student of music, I view North Indian Classical Music not only as an art form, but as a living, evolving language—one that carries centuries of knowledge, emotion, and spiritual depth. My practice as a Sarode player and educator is rooted in the belief that true music has the power to transform, connect, and heal.

Having trained for nearly four decades in the Guru-Shishya Parampara, I have inherited a musical discipline that demands both precision and surrender. But beyond its technical rigor, this tradition has shaped how I approach teaching—with respect for each learner's voice, pace, and cultural context. Whether guiding a student on Sarode or helping a non-Indian instrumentalist translate Indian melodic ideas to the Violin, Cello, or Guitar, my goal is to keep the tradition alive and grow without enclosing it.

The music I perform is almost entirely improvisational—crafted in the moment through deep listening, inner feeling, and subtle responsiveness to time, space, and mood. I explore Raag and rhythm not just as formal structures, but as emotional landscapes, capable of evoking memory, stillness, and release. These values extend into my work as an educator, where I design immersive experiences that engage not just the intellect, but the senses and the self.

Collaboration also plays an essential role in my practice. From working with Grammy-nominated artists to volunteering in the "Children of War: Invisible Wounds" project in Ukraine, I have seen how music can cross borders and serve as a tool for empathy, cultural exchange, and healing.

Now based in Toronto, I continue to perform, teach, and share this tradition with audiences and students—helping them not just to hear the music, but to feel it deeply.

Behind the Music

Creative Process — Performer

"Daily practice is woven into my life. I usually spend 3 to 4 hours with my Sarode, and at least an hour listening to recordings of my Gurus. These two practices feel like breath and heartbeat — they ground me, nourish my inner world, and keep the creative energy flowing."

1

Mental Preparation

My preparation begins a day before. I attune myself to the time of day, the emotional atmosphere of the venue, the expected audience, and my own inner state. I begin imagining a few Raags that may suit the occasion.

2

Choosing the Raag

I choose a Raag based on the kind of mood or spiritual space I want to create. If the audience is new to North Indian classical music, I may select a Raag that is melodically rich but easy to absorb. If it's a musically informed audience, I may explore something deeper or more intricate.

3

Tuning and Centering

I tune my Sarode slowly and carefully. I sit with the Tanpura or in silence to centre myself. This is the moment when the outer world starts to fade and the inner space begins to open.

4

Beginning with Alaap

The performance begins with the Alaap — a slow, unmetered unfolding of the Raag. Nothing is pre-planned. I stay within the Raag's grammar but listen deeply to what wants to emerge in that moment.

5

Moving into Jod and Jhala

As energy builds, I move into Jod (with a steady pulse) and then Jhala (fast and rhythmic). These transitions happen naturally. Years of practice allow me to improvise freely while holding the structure in my body.

6

Composition with Tabla

I conclude the performance with one or more compositions set to different rhythmic cycles, accompanied by Tabla. Even within these fixed compositions, the space for improvisation remains vast. The concert ends when I feel that the emotional or spiritual journey is complete.

Teaching Philosophy

Creative Process — Educator

A six-step approach refined over 20+ years of teaching across cultures, instruments, and conservatories.

1

Getting to Know the Student

I begin by understanding the student's background — their musical exposure, cultural context, and the instrument they've chosen. Whether it's Sarode, Sitar, Rabab, Esraj, Dilruba, voice, or a non-Indian instrument like Violin or Guitar, I approach each with curiosity and care, knowing that every instrument carries its own way of speaking.

2

Creating a Personal Learning Path

Once I have a sense of who the student is, I set a long-term goal (6 to 12 months) and break it down into small, clear steps. Whether I'm teaching in a conservatory setting or one-on-one, I build a structured yet flexible lesson plan that blends technique, theory, and fixed compositions — all tailored to the instrument and the student's pace.

3

Building the Foundation

This is where we focus on the fundamentals — Raag, Taal, ornamentation, and carefully chosen practice materials. I make sure students receive written notations, recordings, and clear explanations. These are the building blocks of their musical language.

4

Supporting Progress

I keep a close eye on each student's development and adapt the material as needed. Corrections are gentle and thoughtful. I want students to feel supported, not judged — the goal is steady growth, not perfection.

5

Introducing Improvisation

Once students have a solid base, usually after 1–2 years, we begin exploring improvisation. I guide them slowly — offering examples and encouraging them to experiment with their own musical ideas. Improvisation, like meditation, can't be forced — only nurtured.

6

Sharing and Reflecting

I regularly organize performance opportunities, whether formal report concerts or informal class sharings. These are moments for students to express, reflect, and grow. Afterward, I reflect too — always looking for ways to improve my approach and stay responsive to each student's journey.
